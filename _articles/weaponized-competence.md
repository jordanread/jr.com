> This is a fictional story. All characters in this story are entirely fictional and any resemblance to actual persons, living or dead, is coincidental, and let's be honest, so unlikely that it almost rises to the level of impossibilty. Prove me wrong, please. I WANT TO BELIEVE.

The nano instance was open in front of her, a git commit message for a merge. Her eyes scanned through the commit messages in her local branch, looking for the snarky comments that nobody should see. The *truth* that nobody should see. She sat back in her chair, rubbing her temples to provide temporary relief of the throbbing that had only gotten more insistent, intense, and painful since "Don't be evil" was intentionally abandoned, a harbinger of things to come.

All those years ago, when she was put on this team, on this project, it was exciting. Technically, sure, it was a camera roll. A chronological view of photos taken from a phone. But then machine learning let her team start figuring out who was in a photo. EXIF data let them start building a heatmap with the users own photos. That bunch of photos from that one vacation, the sunsets from hiking trips on the outskirts of the city people lived in, pictures from events they went to. They were able to consume that data, and create new and interesting ways to display it. In the executive parlance, it was a value-add feature. Value for the user. A nod to them, thanking them for trusting us.

That's not her job anymore though. Same project, but different goals. Instead of giving the user cool tools to do cool things, it shifted to exploiting users, and depending on not only the forced vendor lock-in, but also the technological inertia. Photos was always a thing. How else would I see my pictures? But things got dark. Metrics to determine how long a user looked at a specific kind of picture. That identification functionality turned into ways to push that content to keep people on the app, staring at the screen. Not looking up, not moving on. She remembered holidays with her parents, when they would pull out the shoebox of hardcopies of photos, eliciting a groan and an eyeroll from everyone around. It would never be admitted, but there was always a smile with that groan. There was a *value* intrinsic to each photo in that box.

## GENERATED

The pivot didn't happen all at once. That was the part almost nobody outside the room ever understood — there was no meeting where someone stood up and announced *we're going to make this worse now*. There were only smaller meetings, quarterly ones, where the same chart kept getting reprojected with a slightly different y-axis. Time in app. Session depth. Return frequency. Numbers that used to be a side effect of building something people liked became the target, and the liking became the side effect, if it survived the trip at all.

The heatmaps that used to just be a fun little feature — *look, here's everywhere you've ever taken a picture, isn't that neat* — got repurposed. Not deleted, repurposed, which was somehow worse, because the code that generated them barely had to change. Same pipeline, same embeddings, new consumer. The face-clustering that used to tell you *these forty photos are all your daughter* got wired into a scoring model that told the ad stack *this account skews toward a household with young children, high disposable income bracket, purchases seasonal.* Nobody on her team wrote a line of code that said "build an ad profile." That would've needed a design review, a privacy sign-off, a paper trail. What they wrote, six months at a time, in perfectly reasonable-sounding tickets, was "improve signal quality for the recommendation surface," and by the time you added up eleven quarters of perfectly reasonable tickets, that's what you had. She used to be able to point to the exact commit where the picture had changed. Somewhere past the fourth reorg, she stopped being able to.

Her contribution — if you could even call it that, it felt too small and too petty to deserve the word — was almost embarrassingly simple. She didn't touch the recommendation model. She didn't touch the ad pipeline, couldn't have gotten near it without three approvals and a background check she wasn't sure she'd pass anymore. What she touched was ingestion. The oldest, most boring part of the codebase, the part so foundational nobody had meaningfully reviewed it in years because it was assumed to be *solved*. Photo gets taken. Photo gets uploaded. Photo shows up in your camera roll. That was the whole job, the very first job, the one the product used to be before it was anything else.

She made it flaky. Not broken — broken gets noticed, broken gets a P1 and a war room and her name on an incident report at 2am. Flaky. A photo taken at 11:58pm might not resolve in the roll until the next sync cycle. A burst of ten photos might land nine. Nothing that failed a test, because she'd made sure the tests never checked for it — you can't regress a behavior nobody wrote an assertion for. Users would notice, eventually, the way you notice a draft in a house before you notice the door left open. *Where's the picture I just took?* Some would blame their phone. Some would blame their storage. A few, the sharp ones, would start exporting things "just in case," which was the whole point, if she was honest with herself about the point.

The absurd part — the part that, on her worse nights, made her laugh out loud alone in her apartment — was that when the numbers came back, nobody connected it to her diff. Session length ticked up slightly, because people opened the app more often, hunting for the photo that should have been there. Someone in a readout called it "a promising engagement signal from increased app-open frequency around capture events," and got a small, sincere round of applause. They'd built a metric so disconnected from the thing it was supposed to measure that it couldn't tell the difference between delight and a broken product making someone anxious enough to check twice. She sat in that meeting with her camera off and her hands flat on the desk, and said nothing, because saying something would have meant explaining how she knew.

At night, on a ten-year-old ThinkPad that had never touched a corporate network, she pushed commits to Immich. Different problem, same shape — help people keep the photos that were theirs, on hardware that was theirs, without asking a recommendation model's permission first. Nobody in the readout would ever know her name was on both sets of commits, six hours and one enormous moral distance apart. It wasn't redemption. She was careful with herself about that word too. It was just the one place left where shipping code and doing right by the person on the other end of it were still, occasionally, the same task.

She'd thought about the easter egg for months before she actually built it, turning it over the way you turn over a joke you're not sure lands. It had to be something that would never survive a design review, never show up in a screenshot a PM would circulate, and never, under any circumstances, look like malice if someone did stumble across it in code review. Plausible deniability wasn't optional. It was the whole medium.

She found the gap in the "About" screen, in the little block of legal text nobody read, ever, not once, not even the lawyers who'd approved it, because approving boilerplate was a formality and formalities didn't get read closely. Version number. Build hash. Open-source licenses, scrolling, dense, gray-on-gray, the kind of text specifically designed to be skimmed past on the way to the button that actually did something. She added one more line to that block. Grammatically unremarkable. Formatted exactly like every other line around it — same font, same size, same shade of "nobody cares." A single, real, correctly-attributed open-source license notice, for a library the app genuinely did use — that part wasn't even a lie — except the project name in that one line, if you looked it up, if you were bored enough or curious enough or paranoid enough to actually type it into a search bar instead of scrolling past, resolved to a GitHub mirror whose README opened with three words: try F-Droid instead.

It would never move a single engagement metric. She knew that. Maybe four hundred people, ever, in the history of the build, would care enough about a legal-notices screen to go check what oddly-specific library name was doing in there, and most of those four hundred were probably other engineers doing exactly what she'd done a hundred times herself — reading a changelog nobody asked them to read, out of pure professional nosiness, at 1am, unable to sleep, unable to stop being the kind of person who reads changelogs. But that was fine. That was, in a strange way, the entire point. She wasn't trying to move a market. She was leaving a light on for the four hundred people who already had the instinct to go looking — the same instinct that had once made her open a debug menu on a hand-me-down phone just to see what was in there, the same instinct the whole industry apparently used to have before it decided curiosity was a support burden to be minimized in the name of "reducing user confusion."

Was it too on the nose? Probably. She decided she didn't care. Sabotage nobody could ever prove wasn't really rebellion, not in any way that counted for anything except her own conscience at 2am — but a joke hidden in a legal-notices screen, built entirely out of things that were all individually, technically, defensibly true, felt like something closer to a signature. Not a confession. A signature. The kind you leave on a piece of work you're not allowed to put your actual name on.
