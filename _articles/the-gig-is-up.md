title: "The Gig Is Up"
description: >-
  Standard employment stopped being the default a while ago. On software-engineer unemployment, the AI-layoff boomerang, and the specific discomfort of asking to be paid directly.
tags: [Career, Gig Economy, AI]
date: 2026-07-25
---
Somewhere in the last few years, "job security" quietly moved from something you could reasonably plan around to something you mostly hope for, the way you hope for good weather on a wedding day. It's not gone, exactly. It's just no longer the default assumption underneath everything else — and a lot of us, myself included, have had to rebuild our sense of what a career even looks like around that fact.

Start with the numbers, because they're worth sitting with for a second. Recent computer science graduates are running an unemployment rate somewhere around 6.1% — well above the unemployment rate for college graduates overall, and higher than plenty of majors that don't come with "in-demand STEM field" attached to them. Zoom out to the broader tech workforce and you're still looking at unemployment north of what tech used to consider normal, with tens of thousands of layoffs stacking up every quarter. By some counts, 2026 alone had crossed 180,000 tech job cuts before summer even started, and AI got named as the reason for something like a quarter of them in the spring months.

Here's the part that should bother you more than the raw numbers, though: a meaningful chunk of those AI-attributed layoffs are getting quietly walked back. Ford is the example everyone's been passing around lately — the company let go of a wave of its most experienced engineers, leaned on AI-driven quality inspection to pick up the slack, watched the defect rate climb anyway, and ended up rehiring, re-hiring, or promoting around 350 of exactly the people it had just shown the door. Ford isn't alone in this, either; industry surveys are starting to put a real number on how often "AI replaced this role" turns into "actually, we need that person back" within a year or two. Some analysts are calling it the AI boomerang, which is a cute name for what's actually a pretty rough thing to live through if you were the one who got laid off in the meantime, mortgage payment and all, while the company ran its experiment on your old job.

None of this means the field is dying, or that the ten-year outlook is bad — it mostly isn't. It means something narrower and, in a way, more disorienting: standard employment, the kind with a single employer, a predictable paycheck, and a plan you can build a life around, is no longer something you can just assume you'll have access to whenever you want it. It's an option, sometimes, if you're lucky and well-positioned and the timing works out. It's not the floor everyone gets to stand on anymore.

So people build floors of their own. Which brings me to the actual, slightly ridiculous state of alternative income in 2026.

There is now, as far as I can tell, a dedicated monetization platform for every conceivable unit of human attention. Patreon and Substack if you write. Ko-fi and Buy Me a Coffee if you'd rather the ask feel smaller and less committal — a coffee, not a subscription, as if the psychology of the price point matters more than the math (it does, that's the whole point). GitHub Sponsors if your value-add is code nobody wants to pay for until the day it breaks and suddenly everybody does. Twitch and YouTube memberships if you can be interesting on camera for an hour at a time. Cameo if you'd rather sell forty-five seconds of yourself saying someone's name in a video message. And then there's OnlyFans, which most people still associate with exactly one category of content, but which has quietly become a general-purpose "pay me directly for the thing I make" platform for fitness coaches, chefs, musicians, and — I am not making this up — a genuinely startling number of software people trying to sell tutorials and code walkthroughs, apparently because the brand recognition and payment plumbing are just that good.

I'll be honest: there's something a little slimy about all of it, at first. Not the platforms — the ask. Putting a tip jar in front of people you respect, or people you don't even know, and saying, essentially, *I made something, and I'd like you to pay me for it directly, please* — that triggers a very specific, very uncomfortable flavor of secondhand embarrassment, at least it does for me. It feels adjacent to begging, even when it very much isn't. The discomfort isn't really about the platform or the ask, though. It's about a thing a lot of us were never taught, and have to learn the hard way once the traditional structure stops doing the asking for us: that the value you provide is real, and that getting paid for it directly, without a company sitting in the middle to make the transaction feel more legitimate, isn't a favor anyone's doing you.

That's the actual skill this decade is demanding, underneath all the AI headlines — not just staying technically sharp, though that still matters, but getting comfortable being the one who says *this is worth something, and here's where you can pay for it* without flinching. Companies used to absorb that discomfort on your behalf. A lot of them can't be counted on to anymore, not consistently, not for as long as they used to.

I don't have a tip jar. Not yet, anyway. But I'll admit the idea of setting up the most self-aware, deliberately ridiculous Cameo account possible — charging strangers a modest fee to watch a software engineer say happy birthday in the driest possible tone — has crossed my mind more than once. Mostly as a joke. Mostly.
